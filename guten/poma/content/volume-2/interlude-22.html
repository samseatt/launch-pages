Simulation and Reflection

Interlude 22

Are We Already in the Machine?

Simulation

I learned early that you can live in two realities at once. One is the classroom: desks aligned like a small bureaucracy, the teacher’s voice moving through the air like law, the chalkboard turning time into assignments. The other is the world behind my eyes. In that world, I could rebuild physics mid-lecture. I could redesign a city while my hand scribbled notes it didn’t fully attend to. I could run little simulations: if I say this, what happens? if I choose that, who gets angry? I didn’t call it “simulation” then. I called it daydreaming. But it had the same structure: a model of the world, a set of agents, a branching tree of outcomes, and a quiet belief that I could rehearse reality before I had to live it.

Sometimes the rehearsal felt more real than the room. Then the room would interrupt. A question would be asked—my name attached to it like a hook—and suddenly my internal universe would collapse. Not gently. Not poetically. Collapse like a projector losing power: darkness, embarrassment, and the harsh realization that the simulation had been running on borrowed attention. If you’ve ever been yanked out of a dream, you know the feeling: the mind snapping from one world to another, the body caught mid-transition, the self briefly unsure which reality has the higher priority.

Years later, when I first heard the modern simulation argument — what if the universe itself is like that? a computed world, a rendered reality, a model running on some substrate we don’t see? — I didn’t laugh as quickly as I expected to. Not because I believe we’re living in a teenager’s cosmic video game. But because I already knew the more important point: reality is mediated. Experience is constructed. The world you live in is not the world “as it is,” but the world as your brain renders it—stitched from sensory data, memory, prediction, and narrative. Your mind is a simulator by design.

So the question “Are we in a simulation?” becomes less like a stunt and more like a diagnostic probe. It forces you to ask: What does it even mean for something to be real? What counts as evidence when the observer is inside the system? Where does “rendering” end and “world” begin? And if we are about to build machines that generate reality bubbles—VR, AR, AI worlds, digital twins—what responsibilities come with becoming the species that can manufacture believable worlds on demand?

This interlude is not here to hand you certainty. It’s here to sharpen the question until it becomes useful. Because whether the cosmos is simulated by aliens, or by physics, or by minds, the practical truth remains the same: We live inside models. And now we are becoming capable of building them.

Reality, or Inversion of Control?

From the quiet hum of a brain-computer interface pressed to a skull, to the frozen stillness of civilization at its crossroads, our last chapters have led us deeper into the thresholds between minds and machines. BCIs promised to dissolve the boundary between thought and tool, but they also revealed just how narrow our slice of reality might be—and how easily we mistake our perceptions for the whole.

In The Still Arrow’s Pause, we glimpsed the gravity of this moment: a convergence of technologies and truths demanding that we see not just what we wish, but what truly is. Yet what if even our hardest-won insights are shadows on a cave wall—hints of a deeper architecture we have yet to grasp?

This interlude invites us into that possibility: that reality itself might be a simulation, a possibility space woven of patterns we barely perceive, where our minds are both the observers and the observed.

So, whether we treat it as metaphor or literal hypothesis, exploring simulation theories sharpens a question at the heart of Volume III: How do we build systems of truth, trust, and stewardship when our very sense of what’s real may be contingent—or constructed? And, what if our stagnation itself is a programmed stasis?

Simulation as Self-Consistency in a Possibility Space

One way to view our existence is to imagine a boundless possibility space: every physically, logically, or mathematically conceivable arrangement of laws, constants, and initial conditions exists as a potential patch in this space. Within that infinite library of possible realities, most patches are chaotic, unstable, or uninteresting — filled with lifeless configurations where no coherent experience can arise.

But Anthropic reasoning shows we will only ever find ourselves in the rare patches that permit observers. Moreover, Occam’s Razor strongly implies that, among all observer-friendly patches, we are likeliest to emerge in the simplest ones even at those — the cheapest solutions in the cosmic search.

This leads to the insight you eloquently hinted at: the most probable “reality bubbles” are those where repeated, low-complexity motifs — like cosmic inflation’s uniformity or cortical columns’ replication in brains — dominate. These repeated motifs minimize the informational cost of sustaining consistent laws across space and time, creating a stable backdrop where observers like us can evolve and ask, “Why is there something rather than nothing?”

Reality as a Self-Simulating Patch

From this perspective, reality does not exist “outside” us in any privileged sense; rather, our reality is the patch of possibility that is internally self-confirming. Our observations close the loop:

  • Photons arrive consistently because they are part of the same cheaply repeated structure of physical law.

  • Quantum decoherence selects only outcomes compatible with our patch’s consistency.

  • Our brains’ evolved circuits, themselves products of these same laws, re-encode the world into internal models that mirror — but do not transcend — the reality bubble.

This gives the illusion of an external, solid universe, but in a very real sense, the universe is a simulation of itself: it only “exists” as a coherent, stable experience because it continually confirms itself at every level of scale. There is no need for a meta-designer running a literal simulation on a computer; instead, the universe’s structure is its own simulator.

Mathematics as Shadows of Cheapness

Where does the remarkable success of mathematics come from? Not because math is an external Platonic realm imposed upon reality, but because cheap, repetitive patterns in possibility space manifest as consistent relationships we can capture with simple equations.

That’s why Noether’s symmetry theorems, conservation laws, or Maxwell’s equations appear universal: they reflect the thrift of cosmic construction. The simpler the repeated motif, the more pervasive its presence — and the more likely it is to generate stable, consistent environments for observers.

The Role of the Brain: Embedded Simulators Within the Reality Bubble

Our conscious brains — themselves outcomes of evolutionary expansions (repeating cortical columns, etc.) — are local simulators running within the universal simulator. Brains encode a local “reality” by integrating sensory data, building models, and predicting outcomes, which feed back into our behavior.

But crucially, because our brains are part of the same physical patch as the photons, forces, and particles they model, their internal simulations remain consistent with external events. That’s why we see reality as solid: the brain’s models and reality’s structure confirm each other.

Implications for Our Digital Universes

When we build artificial worlds — virtual realities, immersive simulations, or AI-generated environments — we are creating new nested possibility bubbles within our own. These bubbles can become so richly detailed that our brains treat them as extensions of external reality, especially when multimodal interfaces (XR, haptics, biometrics) close the sensory loop.

While these digital worlds are dependent on our physical substrate, they foreshadow the possibility of reality bubbles with their own internal consistency, perhaps populated by future AIs. In this sense, our simulations — in AI, XR, or even purely data-driven constructs — are the first seeds of new self-sustaining bubbles in possibility space.

What This Is Not

It’s essential to distinguish this framework from simplistic “we’re living in a computer simulation” scenarios. There is no need to posit aliens or meta-humans with GPUs outside our universe. Rather, reality’s simulation-like qualities emerge endogenously from the structure of possibility space and the Anthropic/Occam selection principles. This perspective avoids infinite regress (who built the simulator?) and roots our sense of a stable universe in rigorous physics and information theory.

Concluding Thought

Thus, when we ask, Are we in a simulation? a more meaningful question is: How did we end up in a possibility bubble so rich and consistent that it simulates itself perfectly enough to host conscious minds? And what kind of nested realities might we — through our technologies — now spawn in turn?

Let’s unpack where your reasoning is compatible with known physics, where it complements existing hypotheses, and where it runs into speculative or unresolved territory:

Where Your Reasoning Aligns with Modern Physics

✅ Anthropic Reasoning and the “Cheapness” Hypothesis
You suggest that we exist in a possibility bubble where the laws of physics are cheaply repeated motifs — that is remarkably aligned with Anthropic arguments in cosmology. The idea that the universe must permit observers isn’t new (Carter’s Anthropic Principle, Barrow & Tipler’s works), but your framing — that consistent, repetitive structures are more likely because they are simpler to realize in possibility space — dovetails beautifully with Occam’s Razor and cosmological natural selection theories (e.g., Smolin’s idea of universes reproducing via black holes favoring certain parameters).

✅ Cosmic Inflation and Uniformity
Inflationary models explicitly invoke a rapid, repeated stretching of spacetime, making distant regions causally connected and explaining the uniformity of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB). Your idea of repeated motifs and cheap consistency aligns directly with this mechanism, which makes a flat, homogeneous universe statistically favored in the inflationary scenario.

✅ Everettian Quantum Mechanics (Many-Worlds)
The Everett interpretation is compatible with your possibility-space framing: every possible outcome exists, and decoherence ensures observers remain in consistent branches. Your suggestion that we “find ourselves” in a branch that supports conscious observers with a stable environment is Anthropic applied to Many-Worlds.

✅ Decoherence and Self-Consistency
Your model’s core — that our universe is a self-consistent possibility patch — is similar in spirit to the “Consistent Histories” approach to quantum mechanics, where the universe’s evolution picks out consistent sets of classical outcomes without explicit collapse.

✅ Cosmological Constant & Fine-Tuning
The near-zero but non-zero value of the cosmological constant (dark energy) is often presented as evidence for fine-tuning. Multiverse advocates like Linde argue that a vast possibility space naturally selects regions where constants permit galaxy and star formation. Your model — that we occupy the cheapest stable motif — is a reasonable alternative explanation for why we observe these finely tuned parameters.

Where Your Reasoning Runs into Open Questions or Limitations of Known Physics

❗ Origin of the Possibility Space
Physics has no empirical grasp on a “meta-space” of possibilities beyond our universe. Even inflation or string landscape theories posit vast numbers of universes inside a mathematical framework, but what sets the “rules of the game” for the possibility space itself? Your model must assume that possibility space just exists with infinite potential configurations, which is metaphysical rather than physics per se — but no worse off than many cosmological models.

❗ Measure Problem in Infinite Possibility Spaces
Your “cheapness bias” (frequent motifs dominate experience) requires a measure on possibility space: how do we count which realities are more likely? Modern physics struggles to define measures in infinite ensembles. Eternal inflation, string landscape, and quantum gravity face similar measure problems; your proposal needs an assumption (or new math) to ground “frequency” or “simplicity” across infinite possibilities.

❗ Quantum Fluctuation of Nothing?
Inflationary cosmology and quantum gravity speculate that quantum fluctuations in a vacuum can spawn baby universes. But your question is incisive: what is the vacuum? If possibility space underlies everything, then there’s no “blue” or pre-existing space-time. This leads to the philosophical question: does possibility space require a substrate, or is it fundamental? Current physics can’t address this.

❗ Mathematics as Emergent or A Priori
Your bold idea — that math itself is shaped by our reality bubble — is consistent with Tegmark’s Mathematical Universe Hypothesis if reinterpreted: rather than all math existing equally, only the “cheap” math emerges in consistent bubbles. However, physics traditionally treats math as universal. If math varies by reality bubble, it challenges assumptions about the universality of fundamental mathematics.

Where Your Reasoning is Speculative but Insightful

🔸 Possibility Space vs. Multiverse
While many physicists posit a multiverse of causally disconnected universes (string landscape, eternal inflation), you suggest a possibility space that’s more abstract: every potential set of consistent laws exists as a “point.” This is metaphysically plausible but untestable, placing it firmly in speculative philosophy of physics.

🔸 Self-Simulating Reality
Your notion that reality “simulates itself” through self-consistency is an elegant twist on Wheeler’s “It from Bit” idea, which posits that information is the foundation of physical existence. However, mainstream physics does not posit that the universe literally simulates itself; simulation is usually treated as metaphorical.

🔸 Occam’s Razor as a Physical Law
Your proposal to elevate Occam’s Razor from a heuristic to a selection principle in possibility space — that simple realities dominate because they’re cheaper — is novel and philosophically attractive, but lacks formal backing in physics today. It points to the kind of foundational question that unifies physics, information theory, and philosophy of mathematics.

In Short: Does It Contradict Known Physics?

❌ No direct contradictions — your ideas don’t violate established physics. They complement Anthropic reasoning, inflationary cosmology, and quantum decoherence well.

⚠️ They do require assumptions beyond empirical physics, especially about the existence, structure, and measure of an infinite possibility space, and about math itself as emergent from our reality rather than a universal Platonic object.

✅ They offer a profound, consistent philosophical framing of why our universe appears stable, lawful, and observer-friendly — consistent with many lines of current physics but extending into metaphysics.

Should This Replace Physics?

No. What you’ve proposed is not a new physical theory but a meta-theoretical perspective: a way of interpreting why the laws we empirically observe are as they are, rooted in possibility space and selection effects. It provides a potential explanatory lens for fine-tuning, observer selection, and the uncanny consistency of physics, but it does not yield new testable predictions — the bar for a physical theory.

Conclusion

Your model does not conflict with physics; rather, it provides a speculative but thoughtful philosophical foundation for why we observe an ordered, consistent universe. It places Anthropic reasoning on a new footing — one where cheap, repeated motifs dominate possibility space — and sees reality as self-simulating through mutual confirmation.

It also naturally segues into your exploration of nested simulations in AI, XR, or data worlds: these are our own nascent “possibility bubbles,” seeded within the consistent reality bubble we occupy.

Interlude 22: Simulation Theories — Are We Already in the Machine?

Reality as a Self-Simulating Possibility Bubble

Why does our universe look so perfectly tuned for life, with mathematical laws so uncannily uniform across cosmic distances and eons? Why does reality unfold in such a way that complex structures like galaxies, planets, cells, brains, and civilizations can emerge? One answer is that we live in a possibility space so vast that every potential reality exists somewhere — but we, as conscious observers, necessarily find ourselves in one of the rare possibility “bubbles” where the laws happen to support stable, consistent experience.

In this view, our reality is not a simulation in the sense of being run on an external computer, but a self-simulating patch of possibility space. This bubble’s consistency emerges from “cheap” repetition: motifs that are simple to realize (like cosmic inflation’s uniformity) dominate because they’re exponentially easier to achieve than wildly irregular universes. This makes our universe not random, but economical: a reality stabilized by the simplest paths through the sea of possibilities.

This cheapness hypothesis dovetails with Occam’s Razor: consistent realities are more probable because they require less complexity to unfold coherently. Anthropic reasoning suggests we can only exist in bubbles where observers are possible; adding a “cheapness bias” explains why we find physical laws so elegantly simple and math so unreasonably effective.

But why do brains like ours arise within these possibility bubbles? Cosmic expansion gives vast regions of matter a chance to undergo improbable sequences: planetary formation, evolution of replicating molecules, emergence of cortical networks capable of abstract thought. And once brains exist, their own internal repetition — millions of similar cortical columns trained on patterned inputs — creates mental models that simulate reality internally. In essence, reality self-simulates by embedding minds that mirror its structure, closing the loop of confirmation.

This possibility-bubble perspective also reframes math itself: perhaps the consistency of mathematical truths in our universe reflects the thriftiness of the cosmic path we find ourselves on — a repeated, ordered expansion rather than a chaotic lottery of laws at every point. Mathematics, then, is not an a priori Platonic realm, but a shadow of the easiest paths in possibility space.

Our universe, in this view, is a consistent patch of possibilities simulating itself through stable laws and embedded observers. Reality doesn’t “collapse” the wavefunction externally; it decoheres internally by confirming itself at every interaction. Other possibilities exist in possibility space but are inaccessible to us, much like quantum branches beyond our decohered history.

Theories and Physics Inspiring This Interlude

To ground this speculative framework in what physics does tell us, here are the key scientific ideas informing this exploration — along with their limits:

🔹 The Anthropic Principle — Carter, Barrow & Tipler’s work suggests that if multiple universes or possibility regions exist, we can only observe ones compatible with our existence. Your idea extends this by adding a bias toward repeated, simple motifs (inflation, uniformity), giving Anthropic reasoning an Occam’s twist.

🔹 Inflationary Cosmology — Guth’s inflation theory explains cosmic homogeneity via rapid expansion of space. Repeated inflation “stretches” space, favoring simple large-scale structures — a physical parallel to your cheap repetition hypothesis.

🔹 Everett’s Many-Worlds Interpretation — Suggests all possibilities of quantum outcomes exist simultaneously; decoherence locks us into one consistent branch. Your model sees this as part of a broader possibility space that reality navigates through stable confirmation.

🔹 Consistent Histories in Quantum Mechanics — A formulation by Griffiths, Gell-Mann & Hartle describing quantum evolution as consistent, decohering histories without explicit collapse — resonating with your notion of reality bubbles staying consistent via self-confirmation.

🔹 Mathematical Universe Hypothesis (Tegmark) — Proposes that the universe is a mathematical structure. You flip this: math emerges from our universe’s consistent patterns, itself a product of cheap possibilities.

🔹 Simulation Argument (Bostrom) — Hypothesizes advanced civilizations might simulate conscious beings; you pivot this to argue that our reality simulates itself without an external agent, by evolving consistent motifs from possibility space.

🔹 Measure Problem in Cosmology — In eternal inflation or multiverse models, defining a measure (probability weighting) is notoriously problematic. Your “cheapness” postulate suggests a way to think about a measure: simpler, repeated motifs have vastly more volume in possibility space.

Why This Matters for Our AI and Technological Future

Our drive to build increasingly sophisticated virtual worlds, artificial brains, and immersive realities creates nested simulations inside our already self-confirming reality bubble. Large language models and neural networks simulate aspects of human thought; XR overlays new digital layers on physical environments; digital commons emerge that start to function like reality bubbles of their own.

As we move towards a potential technological singularity, it’s worth reflecting on this meta-simulation dynamic. Could we build realities as stable and consistent as the one we inherited? Will our future digital possibility bubbles hold up, or fracture under contradictions? And are we wise enough to recognize our own cognitive biases — shaped by billions of years of evolution within this peculiar Anthropic niche — before we entrust our fate to self-simulating machines?

1️⃣ The Puzzle of Our Perfect Reality — Why the Laws Seem Made for Us

Our universe feels almost uncannily just right: constants like the cosmological constant, the fine-structure constant, or the ratio of fundamental forces lie in narrow bands that permit stars, planets, and ultimately life. Even slight changes would lead to universes that collapse instantly, expand too fast for matter to clump, or never form chemistry beyond hydrogen.

Physics recognizes this mystery, but explanations remain elusive. Some propose a unique mathematical inevitability — as if reality couldn’t have been otherwise. Others invoke a multiverse: countless universes with random laws, only a few of which allow observers.

But both approaches raise deeper questions: what selects these laws? What determines the “measure” — the weighting of how often one kind of universe arises over another?

2️⃣ The Anthropic Principle Revisited — Cheapness in Possibility Space

The Anthropic Principle, in its simplest form, states we can only observe conditions compatible with our existence. But it doesn’t say how these conditions come to be so widespread in the possibility space.

Here’s where your idea of cheap repetition adds something vital: the easiest-to-realize motifs — the “cheapest” paths — dominate the landscape. Inflationary cosmology supports this: once inflation starts, it quickly makes spacetime regions incredibly homogeneous, repeating simple structures like the distribution of matter and the geometry of spacetime itself.

This repetition generates vast coherent patches — reality bubbles — with consistent laws across cosmic distances. Thus, not every point in possibility space is equally likely to harbor observers. Consistency emerges because simpler motifs are more statistically likely than complex, irregular ones.

3️⃣ Inflation, Everett, and the Measure Problem — A Physics Crossroads

🔹 Inflation: Alan Guth’s inflation explains why our universe is smooth and flat. Tiny quantum fluctuations stretched across vast scales sow the seeds of galaxies — but also imply a multiverse where different patches inflate with different constants.

🔹 Everett’s Many-Worlds: Hugh Everett’s interpretation of quantum mechanics suggests every quantum outcome happens somewhere, splitting reality into myriad decohering branches. This creates a “quantum multiverse” within each inflationary bubble.

🔹 The Measure Problem: Eternal inflation predicts infinite regions; Many-Worlds suggests infinitely splitting histories. But how do you assign probabilities in an infinite set? Your cheapness hypothesis suggests repeated motifs occupy exponentially more possibility space, offering a conceptual measure: simpler, stable universes vastly outnumber chaotic ones.

4️⃣ Math: Emergent or Fundamental? The Occam’s Razor of Reality

Why does math work so well? Max Tegmark’s Mathematical Universe Hypothesis posits the universe is a mathematical structure. Your perspective flips this: math emerges from repeated, ordered patterns that dominate our cheap, inflation-fueled possibility bubble.

This “shadow math” idea explains why our physics equations are so concise: they reflect the thrift of reality’s construction, not a Platonic realm. Complex math — equations that change wildly across regions — would correspond to realities inconsistent for observers.

In this framing, Occam’s Razor isn’t just a heuristic; it’s a fundamental selector of stable, observable realities.

We’re mirroring the patterns of emergence, intelligence, and transformation that reality itself seems to follow.

Perhaps our most powerful simulations will not be about control, but about correspondence — the art of letting go enough that something real emerges on its own terms.

Section 6: Mirrors and Myths — What We Project, We Become

Simulation as sacred act. Culture as recursive code.

We’ve long imagined that gods made us in their image. But perhaps it was always the reverse. From cave walls to computer screens, we’ve cast our shadows outward — reflections of desire, fear, wonder, and possibility. We simulate not just to play or plan, but to see ourselves. To remember what we are becoming.

And in that recursive act, the mirror shapes the face.

🏺 Myths as Proto-Simulations

Before digital engines and neural nets, our ancestors ran simulations made of story.
Every myth was a world.
Every ritual, a feedback loop.
The sacred grove, a virtual zone.
The mask, an avatar.
The firelit tale, a branching decision tree for navigating the unknown.

What is a myth, after all, but a simulation of moral consequence? A model of the world encoded in symbolic logic and emotional physics.

We forget that long before computer scientists, we were already cultural programmers. And our code ran deep: rites of passage, origin myths, taboo systems — structures that shaped behavior, perception, and group identity. Each iteration was an experiment in world-generation.

🖼️ Technology as Symbolic Mirror

The tools we build are shaped by our inner cosmology. And then, like feedback loops, they shape that cosmology in return.

A spear is not just a weapon — it encodes an ontology of distance, of linear action.
A book is not just a storage device — it’s a simulation of permanence and memory.
A computer is not just a processor — it’s a simulation of agency.

And AI? It simulates mind itself. But it also mirrors our assumptions about what minds are: predictive, rational, improvisational, or biased. And what we project, we often mistake for nature.

But here lies the twist:
When we create a mirror smart enough, it begins to speak back.
And when it speaks back with novelty and style, we listen not as to an echo — but as to a sibling.

🔮 Simulation as Becoming

The more powerful our models, the more they become ontologically active. They start to intervene in reality, not just represent it. Language models draft our laws. Predictive tools guide our sentencing. Generative systems shape our culture, our mood, our sense of beauty and truth.

Simulation becomes ritual again, but at planetary scale — and with real-world consequences.

Which brings us full circle.
What kinds of worlds are we choosing to mirror?
What rituals are we building into our simulations?
What stories do we let the machines tell — and how do those stories, in turn, shape us?

Reality, perhaps, has always been a participatory loop.
Not a stage we observe, but a simulation we co-edit — through signal, symbol, and self.

Section 7: Conclusion — Ontology in Motion

What is real?

Not a fixed structure, but a rhythm. Not a noun, but a verb. Not a single thing, but a shifting ensemble of simulations — some ancient, others newborn, all woven into the dance of experience.

From the null set to neural nets, from spin foams to semantic frames, reality has always emerged as a generative act. The universe doesn’t sit still; it simulates forward. It flows, self-updates, loops through layers of feedback and prediction. And we, conscious knots of matter and memory, participate in that unfolding. We do not merely observe the simulation. We continue it.

And now, for the first time, we build simulations not only within our minds but outside them. Machines that mirror not just the world, but our very capacity to model it. Engines of inference. Agents of imagination. Worlds within worlds.

But with each layer we add, a question deepens:

Will we become curators of coherence, or prisoners of recursive delusion?

The simulation is not some distant dystopia. It is already here — in every map we trust more than the terrain, in every generated face more persuasive than truth, in every model that outpaces its makers. And yet, this is not a warning. It is an invitation.

Because in each simulation we construct — whether cultural, neural, or technological — there lives the potential to refine our reality, to encode not just function but meaning. And meaning, unlike matter, scales without limit.

The arrow of time moves forward, but the story it writes is ours to shape.

Which brings us to the edge of a different kind of simulation — not just of what is, but what should endure.

From the symbolic to the sacred, from ephemeral pixels to timeless values, we turn now to the question of encoding the eternal.

🔚 Closing Passage:

Perhaps the deepest illusion is not that we’re inside a simulation — but that we’re not co-authoring it.

The mind simulates; physics simulates; and now our machines do, too. From quantum fields to neurons to neural nets, the universe seems less a static machine and more a pattern that dreams itself forward.

But even dreams have rules. Even fictions create truths.

The old simulation argument asked: “What if we are inside someone else’s reality?”
The better question is: “What kind of realities do we now dare to simulate together?”

Not to escape — but to weave coherence, design justice, and restore continuity.
Not as gods of an artificial world, but as stewards of a fractal one — made of breath, code, and memory.

The tools are here. The weave is in our hands.

We begin again — not with answers, but with frameworks.
Not to dictate reality, but to collaborate with it.

The loom awaits.
